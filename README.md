# Family photo timeline planning workbook

![GrowingUpVideo logo](https://growingupvideo.video/logo.png)

A growing-up film can be a meaningful way to gather family photographs, but the most important work happens before any transition is added. This workbook turns an unorganized collection into a sequence that can be reviewed, corrected, and shared with care. It is designed for families, couples, and people assembling a personal pet timeline. You can complete it on paper, in a spreadsheet, or with a folder of duplicate image files.

GrowingUpVideo is a browser-based tool for arranging portraits by age, previewing their order, and saving a photo timeline. The [grow up ai](https://growingupvideo.video/) resource is one option for planning that sequence. I am associated with the project, and AI assisted in drafting this workbook; it is not an independent review. At the time of writing, the site says film rendering is coming soon and lists one-time transition credit packs. Check current availability, prices, and privacy terms before making decisions.

## Worksheet 1: Define the finished story

Write one sentence describing the audience and occasion. For example, “A private birthday gathering will see a short sequence of portraits selected by the family.” Then list where the result may be shown: a home television, a password-protected family chat, a school event, or a public social account. These uses are different. Someone who agrees to the first may not agree to the last.

Next, set a time range. A timeline might cover childhood through graduation, the years of a friendship, or a pet's first year in a new home. Decide whether the sequence follows one person, a pair, or an animal. Note the desired length and any captions that must be readable from the back of a room. These constraints help you decide how many pictures to keep, but they should not force you to invent missing moments.

Finally, identify a person who can make corrections before sharing. This reviewer could be a family member familiar with the dates or the subject of the film. Ask them to look for incorrect names, unwanted photographs, and sensitive background details. A clear review role prevents the editor from becoming the sole authority over somebody else's story.

## Worksheet 2: Build a source inventory

Make a table with six columns: working filename, approximate date, estimated age, source, permission, and context. A sample row could read: “portrait-04.jpg; around 2010; about eight; family album; private showing agreed; school portrait.” The row is an example, not a claim about an actual photograph. If you do not know a date, leave it uncertain and add a question for a relative rather than guessing a precise day.

Keep a copy of each original in a separate archive. Work on copies so that crops, rotations, and renamed files do not damage the source collection. A scanner may write the date of scanning into the file metadata; that date is not necessarily the date of the scene. Phone exports can also change timestamps. Compare available clues with album notes and documented events before deciding on an order.

Use a simple confidence system if it helps: confirmed, approximate, or unknown. Confirmed might mean a dated event is supported by a reliable caption; approximate might mean the season and year are remembered but not documented. Unknown is a valid answer. Displaying “around age eight” is more honest than presenting an invented date with false precision.

## Worksheet 3: Choose a manageable set

Make a first pass without editing. Mark pictures that are legible and emotionally meaningful, then review the selected set as a group. A strong series can mix formal portraits and everyday scenes. It does not need to show only perfect smiles or evenly staged backgrounds. One image may be technically imperfect but important because it includes a favorite person, animal, or place.

Check for repeated images and long gaps. If a burst of nearly identical photographs occupies half the timeline, keep the one that best serves the story and set the others aside. If a year is missing, do not create a misleading picture to fill it. You can acknowledge the gap in a caption, choose a different time interval, or simply let the sequence move to the next available image.

Look at the sequence with the subject's wishes in mind. Appearance, names, and family relationships may change. Do not assume every available image should be included just because it is chronological. Ask which photographs feel comfortable to share and whether any should be kept only in a private archive. For children, involve a parent or guardian and consider the child's preferences in an age-appropriate way.

## Worksheet 4: Review consent and privacy

For every picture, ask who appears, who owns or supplied it, and what use was agreed. Record whether the image can be used only for a private draft, a family gathering, or wider distribution. A person who allowed an image to be sent to you has not necessarily agreed to an upload or public post. When permission is unclear, choose another image or obtain clarification.

Inspect the background as carefully as the portrait. A school name, house number, location marker, health document, or other identifying detail can appear at the edge of a frame. Crop or blur only on the working copy, and make a note of material removed for privacy. Be careful that the crop does not accidentally make a person look unlike themselves or remove context essential to the story.

Before using any online service, read its current policies for uploaded files, retention, access, and deletion. Avoid uploading the entire family archive when a small selection is sufficient. Use an appropriate access-controlled method for draft review and remove temporary copies when they are no longer needed. Keep the original list so a requested removal can be applied consistently in later versions.

## Worksheet 5: Prepare the visual sequence

Choose an aspect ratio based on the final screen. A wide composition often works well for a television, a vertical composition can suit a phone, and a square may be useful when images will be shared in more than one place. Try the planned framing on several representative images before cropping everything. Portraits with different orientations may need borders rather than aggressive cropping.

Aim for continuity without erasing the photograph's character. Straighten a tilted scan, adjust brightness where needed, and leave some space around the subject. Matching the general position of a face can make a series easier to follow, but do not remove a meaningful background merely to make every frame identical. Old photographs can have a different texture and color palette; that difference can communicate the passage of time.

Add captions only when they improve comprehension. Use readable contrast and allow enough time for the audience to finish reading. If an age or year is uncertain, say so. Try a preview with captions turned off as well: the sequence should still be understandable to someone who knows nothing about the files. If it is not, a brief opening card may be more useful than a paragraph on every photograph.

## Worksheet 6: Decide how motion should be presented

Start by watching the ordered photographs as stills. This reveals mistakes in sequence that a dissolve or animated effect can hide. Then decide whether cuts, gentle fades, or other transitions help the intended story. Avoid implying that an artificial in-between frame is an authentic historical photograph. If an effect creates new imagery, identify it as generated or illustrative when the film is shared.

Pacing is a practical accessibility decision. A complex group image or a caption may need a longer hold than a simple portrait. Preview the result on the device that the audience will actually use. Check that text is not hidden by player controls, and confirm that the visual story still makes sense with the sound muted. If narration is added, provide a text alternative where appropriate.

For music, confirm that the recording can be used in the intended context. A piece suitable for a private family viewing may not be cleared for a public social post. Keep volume changes gentle, and do not treat a soundtrack as a substitute for checking the order and permissions of the images. A quiet presentation can be just as effective as an elaborate one.

## Final review checklist

Before sharing, verify the following:

- The intended audience and the sharing method match the permissions recorded for each image.
- The name, age, date, and relationship captions have been checked, with uncertain details marked honestly.
- The original files remain available separately from edited copies.
- Sensitive addresses, school identifiers, and unrelated people have been reviewed in the background.
- Any generated or substantially altered frames are identified as such.
- The final version plays on the intended screen, and captions are readable at normal viewing distance.
- A person familiar with the story has had an opportunity to request corrections or removal.

Save the inventory beside a dated export and a note describing where it was shared. This makes future changes easier without requiring you to reconstruct every decision. A thoughtful timeline is not measured by the number of transitions it contains; it is a sequence that respects the people in the pictures and makes the chosen story clear.
